This page is my attempt to document all the obscure knowledge I have picked up about UK trains (may occasionally include other countries but if the country is unspecified assume UK). I will try to arrange this information logically so it is easy to look things up but this page will likely be a bit chaotic and I will likely re-order the entire page every now and again. Currently this is a work in progress.

It is almost certain that I have got some information here wrong, feel free to reach out with corrections or other obscure train knowledge.

## Buying tickets
The ticketing system for UK trains is an absolute nightmare, here is my attempt to make sense of it and explain how to end up with the cheapest and most reasonable ticket for a journey most of the time.

### Very short journeys (nice and simple)
If you are travelling a very short distance (one or two stations on a local line), just buy a ticket from your origin to your destination however you want (ticket machines, train operator website etc.). I would discourage using Trainline since they charge a booking fee on top of the ticket price. This is the simplest case and is how I believe the entire ticketing system should work. This is not the case, however, so if you're travelling a longer distance, read the section about longer journeys otherwise you will be absolutely ripped off in terms of ticket price.

### Longer journeys (this is going to take a while)
The TL;DR of buying tickets for a longer journey is use a split fare ticket site, and don't assume any money-saving tricks apply to all journeys.

If you're travelling for a longer distance then it is always worth checking if you can save money by buying a split-fare ticket. But what's a split fare ticket? Glad you asked.

#### Split fare tickets
A split fare ticket is when you buy multiple tickets to cover a single journey. For example, if you're going from A to D, buying three tickets from A to B, B to C and C to D might end up cheaper. Why? I don't think even National Rail has any clue at this point. Doing this is officially allowed according to the National Rail Conditions of Travel (clause 14.2), so you're not breaking any rules.

To buy a split fare ticket, I would recommend using a dedicated split fare ticket site. I use [Split My Fare](https://www.splitmyfare.co.uk/) but you can also use [Trainsplit](https://trainsplit.com/) or [traintimes.org.uk](https://split.traintimes.org.uk/) (doesn't sell tickets). These sites don't charge a fee on top of your ticket price, they take a small percentage of the money you save by splitting your fare. Alternatively, if you don't want to give these sites a small percentage of the savings, you can look up split fares with these sites and buy the combination of tickets manually from a ticket machine or ticket office (can be painful if there are a lot of splits), which is what I tend to do.

Anyway if you just want cheaper tickets and don't care about any other details then just use one of the split fare sites to find tickets and you'll usually get reasonably good offers. If you're interested in more details, read on.

#### Advance vs flexible tickets
Some times, if you look up a journey on a train ticket site, you'll see a lower price labelled as an 'advance' ticket. These are cheaper tickets that are only valid on a specific train and can save you a lot of money if you don't mind the reduction in flexibility. Generally, you'll find good advance ticket offers if the trains you use travel a long distance without you needing to change, and you book at least one week in advance. National Rail says that advance tickets are sold in a limited number but I'm not sure this holds true for all tickets. There are some tickets whose price seems to increase exactly one week before departure and never at any other time so some tickets may just depend on the time until departure. Generally the earlier you book the lower the price will be.

But you can't always save money with advance tickets, that would be too simple. So naturally advance tickets are only available on certain longer journeys and if your journey is long but has lots of changes it's unlikely advance tickets will save you much, even if they do exist for your trains.

If advance tickets don't offer substantial savings or you want more flexibility then you can buy flexible tickets. There are many different types of flexible tickets with different restrictions. How many? Yes.

Don't bother trying to memorise all possible ticket types, they are very specific to the operator you travel with. As for off-peak hours, the only way to find those out is by looking at the restrictions on the off-peak ticket you want to buy (and decipher what it means). Different tickets have different definitions of off-peak because of course they do. A general rule of thumb for this is that peak time is when people generally travel to and from work on weekdays. Weekends are pretty much always off-peak all day. If you're not sure whether to get an off-peak or anytime ticket, I'd say just get an off-peak one unless someone has specifically told you otherwise.

From what I can tell, ticket inspectors are told not to issue fines if the only issue with your ticket is that it's off peak (key word being 'only'). In that case they will simply charge you the difference between your ticket and the cheapest non-off-peak ticket valid on the journey (NRCoT clause 9.5.1). This is not legal advice and I do not endorse deliberately avoiding peak-time fares, this is probably prohibited for some other reason. The key thing is that if you buy an off-peak ticket by accident, you don't need to be worried about being fined. This only applies for flexible tickets, advance tickets are usually only valid on the exact train written on it and you can be fined if you travel on the wrong one.

#### Single vs return tickets
Again, there isn't a hard and fast rule on whether a return ticket will save you money. Most of the time, the price of a Day Return ticket is only a tiny amount more than the price of a single, so the value for money on a return ticket is almost double. If you're leaving and returning on different days, there may or may not be money to be saved by buying a return rather than two singles.

At this point, I treat UK train ticket price calculations as a black box and spend some time looking at all my options every time I'm making a new journey that isn't identical to one I've made before. Any assumption you make will almost certainly be falsified at some point. So don't say things like "always buy advance tickets" or "return tickets are always cheaper".

## Using tickets
It might sound silly for me to explain how to use a train ticket, but there some interesting things to talk about here.

Everything in this section only applies outside of London. London is a whole other mess that requires its own section to explain (this is why I try to avoid travelling through London where possible).

### Breaking your journey
Unless your ticket is an advance ticket or your ticket says you can't, you are permitted to break your journey. This means you can get off at any station between your origin and destination and then continuing your journey. There are some exceptions to this e.g. on circular train lines but they are rare.

Knowing about this is really useful if you're travelling for fun, since you can use one Day Return ticket to explore the areas around multiple stations. This is a huge reason why I often go with flexible tickets over Advance ones.

### Routes you may use
Working out which routes you can use with your ticket is, as you may have guessed, really complicated. There is a National Routeing Guide which defines what is and isn't a valid route and people apparently find ridiculous loopholes in it (if you know how to find loopholes let me know). I can't work out how to use this guide meaningfully so I honestly wouldn't bother trying. Also, National Rail defines any direct train journey or any journey an official train ticket website shows you as valid regardless of what the guide says.

I usually just look at a map of train routes in Great Britain and try inputting all the sensible routes into a ticket site to see what the price comes to.

## London
If you've somehow understood most of the rules for making a train journey outside London then brace yourself, London is basically a train network inside a train network with its own special rules which apply in combination with the rest of UK train rules.

## Compensation
If your train is delayed by 30 minutes or more (or in some cases 15 minutes, of course there are exceptions), you can apply to get delay repay. This sometimes allows you to get part or all of your ticket price refunded to you (excluding booking fees or share of split saving). All you have to do is create a dedicated delay repay account for each train operator that ever delays you (as always, very convenient and totally not at all frustrating).

If your train is delayed and your ticket wasn't very cheap **always apply for delay repay**. In my experience I have been given at least some money back every time I applied for it after a delay of more than 30 minutes. I've overheard many conversations on board a delayed train where people say things like "delay repay isn't worth applying for, it won't give you any money anyway". This is not true, I've had expensive tickets fully refunded with delay repay. Ideally the process for applying for delay repay should be simplified so people don't have to go through all that bureaucracy, but the complexity is probably why delay repay currently works well for those who bother to apply. It makes sense for a train company to compensate those who bothered to fill in the form, since most people don't even bother.
